At 2026 Facebook lead-ad benchmarks, $5,000 a month produces roughly **80 to 360 leads** for a service business — the range is that wide because cost per lead swings from about $14 in real estate to $62 in dentistry. But the lead count is the wrong number to plan around. The figure that decides whether that $5,000 was worth spending is how many **customers** those leads become — usually a single-digit handful — and that depends on your conversion chain, not your ad budget.

## The short answer: it depends on your cost per lead

Divide your monthly spend by your cost per lead and you get your lead volume. At the 2026 all-industry average Facebook cost per lead of **$27.39**, $5,000 buys about **183 leads** a month. But "average" hides an enormous spread. Here's what the same $5,000 produces across industries, using 2026 Facebook lead-ad benchmarks from LocaliQ:

| Industry | Avg. cost per lead (2026) | Leads from $5,000/mo |
|---|---|---|
| Real Estate | $13.74 | ~364 |
| Health & Fitness | $27.11 | ~184 |
| All-industry average | $27.39 | ~183 |
| Personal Services | $38.09 | ~131 |
| Home & Home Improvement | $42.95 | ~116 |
| Dentists & Dental Services | $61.56 | ~81 |

*Source: LocaliQ 2026 Facebook Ads Benchmarks (lead-campaign objective), updated September 2026.*

So the honest answer to "how many leads should $5,000 produce?" is: somewhere between about 80 and 360, and you can't narrow it without knowing your industry, your offer, and whether you're using instant forms or a landing page. But here's the more important point — none of those lead numbers tells you whether the spend worked.

## Why the number of leads is the wrong question

Because leads aren't the thing you're buying. Customers are. And the relationship between the two isn't fixed — it runs through a chain of conversion steps, each of which quietly subtracts.

A lead is just a captured contact. Between that contact and a paying customer sit five more events: the lead has to book a call, show up to it, be genuinely qualified, get closed, and pay. Miss on any of those and the lead produces nothing, no matter how cheaply you acquired it. Two businesses can run the identical $5,000 campaign, produce the identical 183 leads, and end the month with wildly different revenue — because the difference was never in the leads. It was in what happened after.

That's why "how many leads should I get?" is a question that feels precise but answers nothing. The precise, useful question is: **how many customers should $5,000 produce?**

## How to turn an ad budget into an expected number of customers

Run your lead volume through the revenue chain. Every service business's acquisition works as a sequence of multipliers:

**leads × booking rate × show rate × qualification rate × close rate = customers**

Here's a worked example on the all-industry average — 183 leads from $5,000. The rates below are illustrative, not benchmarks; your own will differ, which is exactly the point:

| Stage | Rate (illustrative) | Count |
|---|---|---|
| Leads from $5,000 | — | 183 |
| Booked a call | 40% | 73 |
| Showed up | 60% | 44 |
| Qualified | 50% | 22 |
| Closed | 30% | ~7 |

Seven customers from $5,000 in spend. That works out to a customer acquisition cost of roughly **$715** — and whether that's a triumph or a disaster depends entirely on what a customer is worth to you. For a dentist or a kitchen-and-bathroom firm with a four-figure average job, seven customers for $5,000 is excellent. For a business selling a $200 service, it's a loss.

Notice what happened to the lead count in that calculation: it became almost irrelevant. What decided the outcome was the chain. Lift the booking rate from 40% to 55%, or the show rate from 60% to 75%, and you add customers without spending another pound on ads. You can run your own numbers through [the revenue leak calculator](/revenue-leak-calculator) to see which stage is costing you the most.

## Cheaper leads don't mean more customers

This is the trap the benchmark table sets. Looking at it, real estate's $13.74 cost per lead looks like the best deal on the board and dentistry's $61.56 looks like the worst. But cost per lead and cost per customer are different numbers, and only the second one pays your bills. A flood of cheap leads with a 10% qualification rate can cost more per customer than a trickle of expensive ones that qualify at 60%.

I saw this directly with a client, Compare Funerals. When we optimised their campaigns, the cost per *raw* lead actually got worse on some metrics — but the cost per *qualified* lead fell 71%, from around £521 to £148, and qualified volume went from roughly 24 to 60 a month, on nearly 30% less ad spend. The cheap-lead scoreboard would have called that a failure. The customer scoreboard called it a turnaround. (I broke down why in [cost per lead vs cost per customer](/blog/cpl-vs-cac).)

## What actually changes how many customers $5,000 produces

Four levers move the customer number far more than the lead number does:

- **Speed to lead.** Response-time research across 5.7 million leads found contacting a lead within five minutes produced roughly 8x the conversion of waiting longer — yet most businesses take days. This single lever can double your show rate for free.
- **Qualification.** Tighter targeting and a real qualification step mean your sales team spends its hours on people who can actually buy, lifting close rate.
- **Follow-up.** Most leads don't convert on first contact. A nurture sequence recovers the ones a single call would have lost.
- **Offer and message match.** If the ad promised one thing and the call delivers another, no amount of lead volume fixes it.

Spend $5,000 on ads with these four dialled in and seven customers can become twelve. Spend the same $5,000 with them broken and you'll get the leads and wonder where the revenue went. That gap — same spend, same leads, different outcome — is the whole game, and it's the argument against ["just spend more on ads"](/blog/spending-more-on-ads-wrong-fix) as a growth plan.

## When $5,000/month is the wrong budget to start with

Two honest caveats. If you're **below roughly $3,000–$5,000 a month**, Facebook's algorithm often can't gather enough conversion data to optimise, and management overhead eats the returns — you may be better served by owned channels first. And if your **sales process can't yet convert leads into customers** — no fast follow-up, no qualification, an unproven offer — then $5,000 in ad spend will faithfully deliver 183 leads and roughly zero extra revenue, because the budget multiplies a system that doesn't work yet. Fix the chain first; then the ad spend has something to flow into. ## Frequently asked questions

### How many leads will $5,000 in Facebook ads get me?

At the 2026 all-industry average cost per lead of $27.39, about 183 leads a month. The real range runs from roughly 80 leads (high-cost industries like dentistry at ~$62 per lead) to over 360 (low-cost industries like real estate at ~$14 per lead). Instant lead forms usually produce more, cheaper leads than landing pages, but landing-page leads tend to be better qualified.

### How many customers should $5,000 in ad spend produce?

For most established service businesses, a single-digit handful — often around 5 to 10 — once leads pass through booking, show, qualification and close rates. The exact number depends entirely on your conversion chain, not your budget. The useful way to plan is to estimate your own rates at each stage and multiply them against your expected lead volume, rather than planning around the lead count alone.

### Is $5,000 a month enough for Facebook ads?

For most service businesses, yes — $5,000 is generally above the threshold where Meta's algorithm can gather enough conversion data to optimise, which tends to sit around $3,000 or more per month. Below that, the platform struggles to learn and management costs weigh heavily. Whether $5,000 is enough to hit your growth target, though, depends on your cost per customer, not your cost per lead.

### Why am I getting leads from Facebook but no sales?

Almost always because the leak is downstream of the ad. The leads arrive, but slow follow-up lets them go cold, weak qualification fills the calendar with people who can't buy, or the offer doesn't match what the ad promised. The ad account can look healthy while the business makes no more money — which is why you track customers and cost per customer, not just leads and cost per lead.

### What is a good cost per lead for a service business in 2026?

There's no universal "good" number — it's industry-specific. The 2026 all-industry Facebook average is $27.39, but a good cost per lead for real estate (~$14) would be exceptional for dentistry (~$62). More importantly, a good cost per lead is one that produces a cost per *customer* below what a customer is worth to you. A high cost per lead that closes well beats a low one that doesn't.
