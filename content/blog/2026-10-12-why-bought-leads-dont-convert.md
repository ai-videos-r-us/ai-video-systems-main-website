Bought leads convert poorly for four reasons, usually in this order: they're shared with other buyers, they were captured at low intent, they get a slow or missing reply, and nobody prepared the person to want your service before you called. The fix isn't a better vendor. It's finding which of the four is costing you in your own numbers, then sending that result back to wherever the lead came from.

## Why don't bought leads convert?

A bought lead is a stranger who filled in a form for someone else's reasons. Four things work against you at once, and they stack:

| # | Cause | What it looks like in your numbers | The test | The fix |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Shared** | You reach people who've already spoken to someone | Ask your next ten "who else have you spoken to?" | Exclusive leads, or reach them first |
| 2 | **Low intent** | You reach them and nothing starts: "just looking", "wrong service" | Submit the vendor's form yourself, then call ten leads | Change the source, or the qualification |
| 3 | **Slow or missing reply** | A large share are never reached at all | Minutes from arrival to first attempt, and attempts per lead | An owner, an instant first message, a follow-up run |
| 4 | **Unprepared** | They answer but have no idea who you are | Listen to the first 60 seconds of five calls | Send proof before the call; qualify before it too |

Two of these belong to the vendor and two belong to you. That split matters, because it decides whether the answer is to change where you buy or to change what you do in the minutes after the lead lands. The common mistake is switching vendor and carrying the same result across, because the leak was cause 3 and it travels with you.

## Cause 1: are the leads shared with other buyers?

If the same enquiry went to several businesses, you're not selling to a prospect. You're racing for one. The arithmetic is in [buying leads vs generating your own](/blog/buying-vs-generating-leads): with N buyers the average win rate is one in N before speed counts. Here the point is what it feels like from the inside: you reach the person and they say "someone's already been round" or "I've got two quotes".

**The test.** Ask your next ten reached leads who else they've spoken to, and note the answers. It takes a week and it's the only reliable way to know, because a vendor's description of "exclusive" or "limited" is a marketing claim until you've checked.

**The fix.** Pay for exclusivity if the price still clears your ceiling (the break-even maths is in [what lead generation costs](/blog/lead-generation-cost)), or accept you're in a race and win it on speed, which is cause 3.

## Cause 2: was the lead low intent to start with?

Some leads are made by a form built to get submitted, not to find a buyer. A homeowner who wanted a price range for a kitchen has not asked you to come and quote. A person who ticked a box to "get quotes" on a comparison page is at an earlier stage than one who phoned you after finding you themselves.

**The test.** Submit the vendor's own form, as a customer would, and notice what it asks and what it promises. Then call ten leads and count how many actually start a conversation about the work. If the answers are mostly "just looking" or "I was only after a price", the lead was low intent. No amount of better follow-up changes who they were when they filled it in.

**The fix.** Tighten what counts as a lead in writing before you pay for one: service, area, timing and budget fields at minimum. Or move to a source where the person had to do more to reach you. A short form that asks "when do you need this done?" does more for your close rate than a long one that asks nothing.

## Cause 3: how fast, and how many times, do you actually reply?

This is the one most in your hands, and the one with the cleanest evidence. The 2021 InsideSales study of inbound marketing leads (5.7 million leads, 55 million sales activities, 400+ companies) found:

- Conversion rates were **8 times higher** when the first call came within five minutes than at six minutes or later.
- Under 1% of first attempts happened within five minutes.
- **77% of leads were never responded to at all.**
- 57.1% of attempts waited longer than a week.
- Seven or more follow-up attempts yielded **15% more connections**, yet 81% of sellers made five or fewer.

One honest caveat: that dataset is business-to-business inbound leads, so treat the percentages as the direction of the effect, not a number to expect in a kitchen fitter's diary. The direction is not in doubt. The full set of mechanisms is in [the five-minute rule](/blog/speed-to-lead-5-minute-rule).

**The test.** For your last 20 bought leads, write down the minutes between the lead arriving and your first real attempt, and how many attempts each got. Don't count an automatic confirmation. If the median is hours and most leads get one or two attempts, that's where the money is going.

**The fix.** One named person owns first contact. An instant first message goes out within seconds, and a human attempt follows inside minutes in working hours. Then run a defined sequence over several days rather than a single call and a shrug. Rules on automated calls and texts differ by country, and a bought lead may not have agreed to being contacted that way, so confirm what the lead consented to before you automate anything.

## Cause 4: did anyone prepare them to want you?

A lead from your own content has already watched you explain the problem, seen a result, and seen how you work. A bought lead has seen nothing. Your first call has to do the job your content would have done, from zero, while the person is checking whether to stay on the line. You'll recognise it as the first twenty minutes of every call spent explaining what you do.

**The test.** Listen to the first 60 seconds of five recorded calls or voicemails. If most of it is you introducing the company, you're doing cause 4's work live.

**The fix.** Send proof the moment the lead arrives: a short video or case study, a client story told with its limits, a plain "here's what happens next". Qualify with two or three questions before the call so it starts from fit. This is the same logic as [video doing part of the sales call before the call](/blog/ai-video-to-sales): the lead who has seen you for two minutes is a different lead from the one who hasn't. It's also why [paid-ads customers can close like referrals](/guides/paid-ads-customers-as-good-as-referrals) when something prepared them first.

## What's the fix nobody runs? A loss-reason ledger

Every vendor will tell you what a lead cost. None of them will tell you why yours didn't close, and you probably haven't written it down either. That's the gap. The one fix that makes the other four measurable is a ledger of lost bought leads, filled in as each one drops out, with a single reason from this list:

| Reason | Cause it points to |
|---|---|
| Someone else got there first / "I have other quotes" | 1: shared |
| Wrong service, wrong area, "just looking", not ready | 2: low intent |
| Never reached after the full sequence | 3: reply (or a bad number) |
| Reached but didn't know who we were, or why us | 4: unprepared |
| Reached, qualified, and chose someone else on price or fit | None of the four: it's your offer or your sales call |

After 40 or 50 leads the shape appears. If "someone else got there first" dominates, the vendor's model is the problem, and you should be buying exclusive or buying elsewhere. If "never reached" dominates, your process is the problem and no new vendor will help. If the last row dominates, you've had a lead problem all along and it was never a lead problem.

Then close the loop with the source. Where a platform allows it, report the bad ones: Google's Local Services Ads, for instance, charges you for valid leads, may credit leads it later judges to be duplicate, invalid or poor quality, and invites you to flag poor leads through a feedback survey. Credits aren't available in every country or category, so check your own terms. The credit is the small prize. The big one is that your own numbers, per source, tell you where the next dollar should go. [Lead problem or sales follow-up problem](/guides/lead-problem-or-sales-follow-up-problem) is the companion diagnostic, and the [Revenue Leak Calculator](/revenue-leak-calculator) turns the stage rates into a figure.

## When bought leads convert fine

Some categories produce high-intent leads by nature: the person phoned you because the boiler stopped. A call-in lead for something urgent, from a source where only you were contacted, doesn't have a low-intent problem, and the evidence above applies to you mostly through cause 3. Likewise, if you're early and testing whether a market buys at all, a mediocre conversion rate from bought leads is information, not a failure. The leak only becomes expensive when you're building the business on it. [How to get leads for your business](/blog/how-to-get-leads-for-my-business) puts bought sources in context.

## When should you stop buying?

When you've fixed cause 3, run the ledger for 60 to 90 days, and your cost per customer from the source is still above what a customer can bear. That's the point at which the problem is the source. Use the break-even close rate from [lead generation cost](/blog/lead-generation-cost): the price per lead divided by the most you can pay for a customer. If the source can't reach that number with fast follow-up and proper tracking behind it, it's the wrong source for your business, whichever vendor it is.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why don't bought leads convert?

Usually because of some mix of four things: the lead was shared with other buyers, it was captured at low intent, you replied slowly or not at all, or the person had no reason to want you before you called. Two of those belong to the vendor and two to you. A ledger of why each lead was lost tells you which, and the fix is different for each.

### How fast should I call a bought lead?

Within five minutes, in working hours. In InsideSales' 2021 study of 5.7 million inbound leads, conversion was 8 times higher when the first call came within five minutes than at six minutes or later, and fewer than 1% of first attempts happened that fast. The data is business-to-business, so use it as the direction of the effect.

### How many times should I try to reach a bought lead?

More than most people do. The same study found 81% of sellers made five or fewer follow-up attempts, while seven or more attempts produced 15% more connections. Spread them over several days and several times of day, and check what the lead agreed to before you automate calls or texts.

### Can I get a refund for bad leads?

Sometimes. Google's Local Services Ads states you're charged for valid leads and that poor-quality, duplicate or invalid leads may be credited, automatically or after you report them in a feedback survey, with exclusions for some regions and categories. Other marketplaces have their own terms. Read yours, and record every bad lead in the ledger either way.

### Are bought leads worth it at all?

Sometimes. A bought source is worth it when its cost per signed customer sits below what a customer is worth to you, and that needs every lead tracked to a sale. [Buying leads vs generating your own](/blog/buying-vs-generating-leads) sets out the economics and when each wins.
