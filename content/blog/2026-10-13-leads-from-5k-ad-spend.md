At 2026 averages, $5,000 a month in ads buys roughly 180 leads on Meta or 75 on Google search. Industry moves that a long way: about 38 on Google for legal, about 360 on Meta for real estate. But leads are the wrong unit. At a lead-to-customer rate of 2.8%, $5,000 produces somewhere between 1 and 10 customers a month, and whether that's good depends on what a customer is worth to you.

## How many leads does $5,000 a month buy?

Divide the budget by the cost per lead. That's the whole calculation, and the only hard part is knowing which cost per lead to use. These are the latest published averages, for advertisers running lead campaigns, and the leads they count are form fills and contacts, not conversations:

| Platform and industry | Average cost per lead | Leads from $5,000 |
|---|---|---|
| Meta, all industries (2026) | $27.39 | ~183 |
| Meta, home and home improvement | $42.95 | ~116 |
| Meta, real estate | $13.74 | ~364 |
| Google search, all industries (2026) | $66.69 | ~75 |
| Google search, home and home improvement | $90.92 | ~55 |
| Google search, finance and insurance | $74.44 | ~67 |
| Google search, real estate | $102.51 | ~49 |
| Google search, attorneys and legal | $131.63 | ~38 |

Three cautions. These are averages across many accounts, so your market, offer and creative will land above or below them. The budget is media only: any agency fee or in-house cost comes on top. And in my experience the first two to three months of a new account are partly a data cost, so month one is rarely at the average.

Benchmarks also move. The 2025 all-industry Meta average of $27.66 was itself up 21% on the year before; the 2026 figure of $27.39 is roughly flat. Use the current edition, not a number from an old benchmark post.

## How many customers is that?

Multiply leads by the share that become customers. That's the number that decides whether $5,000 is a good month or a wasteful one, and it's the one no benchmark can give you, because it depends on your response time, qualification and sales process.

For reference, I've used two chains from [how to calculate your revenue leak](/blog/calculate-revenue-leak): a chain most founders would call fine (40% book, 70% show, 50% qualified, 20% close), which is **2.8%** of leads becoming customers, and the Attention-to-Revenue System's target chain (50%, 80%, 60%, 25%), which is **6%**. Those are AVS's model rates, not industry averages. Your own chain may be higher or lower.

| Source | Leads | Customers at 2.8% | Cost per customer | Customers at 6% | Cost per customer |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Meta, all industries | ~183 | 5.1 | $978 | 11.0 | $456 |
| Meta, home improvement | ~116 | 3.3 | $1,534 | 7.0 | $716 |
| Google, all industries | ~75 | 2.1 | $2,382 | 4.5 | $1,112 |
| Google, home improvement | ~55 | 1.5 | $3,247 | 3.3 | $1,515 |
| Google, legal | ~38 | 1.1 | $4,701 | 2.3 | $2,194 |

Read down the columns and the question changes. It isn't "how many leads does $5,000 buy?" It's "how many customers, at what cost each, and does that clear what a customer is worth?" The same budget produces anywhere from about one customer to about eleven, depending on where you advertise and how well the system after the click converts.

## Does that clear what a customer is worth?

Set a ceiling first. A working rule from [my marketing cost post](/blog/service-business-marketing-cost): cost per customer no higher than a third of first-year gross profit. Take an illustrative kitchen fitter or remodeller: an $18,000 project at 30% margin is $5,400 gross profit, so the ceiling is **$1,800**.

- **Meta, home improvement at 2.8%:** $1,534 per customer. It clears the ceiling, with $266 to spare.
- **Google, home improvement at 2.8%:** $3,247. It doesn't clear, and needs a lead-to-customer rate of about 5.1% (price per lead $90.92 ÷ $1,800) to break even. That's roughly one lead in twenty.
- **Either, at 6%:** both clear: $716 on Meta and $1,515 on Google.

The decision isn't "Meta beats Google". Google search leads are people who were already looking, and the cost reflects that; they may close better. What the table gives you is the rate each source needs to hit, which you can then check against your own numbers. If you don't have them, that's the finding. The [cost per customer calculator](/tools/cost-per-customer-calculator) does the division with yours.

## Why does the lead count hide so much?

Because a lead is where the reporting stops, and the money moves after it. Compare Funerals, a UK funeral-plan provider serving British expatriates in Spain (figures in pounds), was a vivid case. Before we started, its Meta cost per lead was £21.58, which looks healthy. Its cost per *qualified* lead was £521.07, about 24 times higher. In the first 30 days, Meta cost per lead fell to £9.63 and cost per qualified lead fell to £147.65, on 29.5% less spend. The lead price moved, but the number that mattered moved much further. It's an acquisition-efficiency result, not a revenue case study.

What sits between a lead and a customer is where most of the loss is, and it's the numbers founders tend to avoid: how fast the first reply is, what share of leads are reached at all, and what share get a second attempt. In InsideSales' 2021 study of 5.7 million inbound leads, 77% were never responded to at all, under 1% of first attempts happened within five minutes, and conversion was 8 times higher when the first call came inside five minutes. That dataset is business-to-business, so read it as the direction of the effect. The direction is the point: **a budget that produces 183 leads and 11 customers and a budget that produces 183 leads and 3 customers look identical on the dashboard.**

If your dashboard shows leads and cost per lead only, you can't tell which one you are. That's why [cost per lead and cost per customer](/blog/cpl-vs-cac) are different numbers, and why [spending more on ads is usually the wrong fix](/blog/spending-more-on-ads-wrong-fix) when the leak is downstream.

## What should $5,000 a month actually do?

Work backwards from what you need, not forwards from what the platform says.

1. **Decide the customers you need a month** from paid, net of what referrals already bring.
2. **Take your tracked lead-to-customer rate.** From the last 90 days, per source. Not a benchmark.
3. **Divide:** leads needed = customers ÷ rate. At 2.8%, one customer needs about 36 leads. At 6%, about 17.
4. **Multiply by cost per lead.** That's the budget that source needs to produce the customers, before fees.
5. **Compare it with your ceiling.** If it clears, spend. If it doesn't, the constraint is the rate, not the budget.

The [Revenue Leak Calculator](/revenue-leak-calculator) runs steps two to five from seven numbers you already know and shows which stage between lead and customer is costing you most.

## When is $5,000 the wrong budget?

- **When it's too little to learn from.** Below roughly $3,000 to $5,000 a month in media, a new campaign rarely produces enough volume to read. [The spend floor guide](/guides/how-much-to-spend-on-ads-before-hiring-a-lead-generation-agency) explains why.
- **When you can't track a lead to a sale.** You'd be buying numbers you can't interpret.
- **When nobody replies within minutes.** More leads of any cost go cold in the same inbox.
- **When referrals already fill the diary.** Then the problem isn't lead volume, and $5,000 is better held back until it is.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many leads should $5,000 a month in Facebook ad spend produce for a service business?

Roughly 180 at the 2026 all-industry average cost per lead of $27.39 on Meta, with a range from about 116 in home improvement ($42.95) to about 364 in real estate ($13.74). That's media only; fees are extra. But leads are the wrong unit. At a 2.8% lead-to-customer rate, 183 leads becomes about 5 customers, and at 6% about 11.

### What is a good cost per lead for Facebook ads?

One that sits under your break-even for the way your leads actually close. Divide the price per lead by the most you can afford to pay for a customer. At an $1,800 ceiling, a $42.95 lead needs about 2.4% of leads to buy. A cheap lead that never closes is the most expensive thing you can buy.

### How many leads do I need to get one customer?

Divide one by your lead-to-customer rate. At 2.8% it's about 36 leads, at 6% about 17, at 10% ten. Don't use a benchmark for the rate. Pull your last 90 days, count the customers each source produced and divide.

### Is $5,000 a month enough to run ads for a service business?

It's about the level where paid ads start to produce enough volume to read, which is why [the spend floor guide](/guides/how-much-to-spend-on-ads-before-hiring-a-lead-generation-agency) puts the floor at $3,000 to $5,000 a month in media. Whether it's enough to be worth it depends on the cost per customer against what a customer is worth, not on the budget alone.

### Why are my leads cheap but I'm not getting customers?

Because the cost per lead measures how cheaply you collected a form fill, not whether the person wanted to buy. Low-intent leads are cheap for a reason. Look at the stages after the lead: reply time, qualification, booking and close. The [leak formula](/blog/calculate-revenue-leak) shows which one is costing you.
